--- title: Open-position colour chords order: 50 summary: Turn each cowboy chord into its sus, 7, maj7, add9 and 6 relatives by moving one finger — and know which finger, and why. level: 1 ---

You already own most of the chords in this chapter. Each open chord has a handful of colour versions one finger away, and they all follow from Chapter 3: find the string that plays the 3rd and the strings that double the root. Move the 3rd and you change the chord's type; move a spare root and you add a colour on top.

The two rules

  • Move the 3rd (usually the only one in the chord) up a half or whole step and you get sus4; down to the 2nd and you get sus2. The chord stops being major or minor.
  • Move a doubled root and the chord keeps its identity — another root is still in the bass — but gains a new note. Down a whole step: ♭7 (a 7 chord). Down a half step: 7 (maj7). Down three half steps: 6. Up a whole step: 9 (add9).

The 5th can move too: up a whole step it becomes the 6.

The A family

A (x02220): root on strings 5 and 3, 3rd on string 2.

{{chords: A_open, Asus2_open, Asus4_open, A7_open, Amaj7_open, A6_open, Aadd9_open, A7sus4_open}}
  • Asus2 / Asus4: the 3rd on string 2 drops to open B (2) or rises to D at fret 3 (4).
  • A7: the root on string 3 drops to open G — the ♭7. Amaj7: it drops one fret to G♯ — the 7.
  • A6: the open top string (5th) rises to F♯. Barring all four strings at fret 2 is the quickest way.
  • Aadd9: the string-3 root rises to B at fret 4.

Asus4 → A → Asus2 → A on one strum each is the most-used colour move in rock rhythm guitar. It's the 3rd being pushed up and pulled down.

The D family

D (xx0232): root on strings 4 and 2, 3rd (F♯) on string 1.

{{chords: D_open, Dsus2_open, Dsus4_open, D7_open, Dmaj7_open, D6_open, D7sus4_open}}
  • Dsus2 / Dsus4: the top-string 3rd goes to open E or up to G at fret 3.
  • D7: the string-2 root drops from D to C (fret 1). The fingers rearrange, but only one note changed.
  • Dmaj7: the same root drops to C♯ — fret 2, so one finger can barre strings 3–1.
  • D6: the root drops all the way to open B.

The E family

E (022100): roots on strings 6, 4 and 1; 3rd (G♯) on string 3 alone.

{{chords: E_open, Esus4_open, E7_open, Emaj7_open, E6_open, Eadd9_open, E7sus4_open, E9_open}}
  • Esus4: the 3rd on string 3 rises from fret 1 to fret 2 (A).
  • E7: the string-4 root drops to open D. Emaj7: it drops to D♯ at fret 1.
  • E6: string 2 (the 5th, B) rises to C♯ at fret 2.
  • Eadd9: the string-4 root rises to F♯ at fret 4 — a stretch from the G♯ at fret 1.
  • E9: E7 plus F♯ on the top string. Funk's favourite open chord.

The G family

G (320003): roots on strings 6, 3 and 1; 3rds on strings 5 and 2.

{{chords: G_open, G_open2, G7_open, Gmaj7_open, G6_open, Gsus4_open, Gsus2_open, Gadd9_open}}
  • G7, Gmaj7, G6: the top-string root drops to F (fret 1), F♯ (fret 2), or open E. Same string, three colours.
  • Gsus4: both 3rds (B) become C — string 5 to fret 3, string 2 to fret 1.
  • Gsus2: start from the rock G (320033) and lift the string-5 3rd: the open A replaces B. Gadd9 keeps the 3rd and adds the A on string 3 instead.
  • G with D on top (320033): the second G above swaps the open B for D at fret 3 of string 2. It's the version to learn, because fingers 3 and 4 then stay put for Cadd9, Dsus4 and Em7.

The Beatles end “She Loves You” on a G6 — the open-top-string version is that sound.

The C family

C (x32010): roots on strings 5 and 2, 3rds on strings 4 and 1.

{{chords: C_open, Cmaj7_open, C7_open, C6_open, Cadd9_open, Csus2_open, Csus4_open, Fmaj7_open}}
  • Cmaj7: lift finger 1. The string-2 root falls to open B.
  • C7: add the little finger on B♭ (string 3, fret 3). The 5th goes; nobody notices.
  • C6: string 3 (the 5th) rises to A at fret 2.
  • Cadd9: the G-family shape. Fingers 3 and 4 on fret 3 of strings 2 and 1 play D and G. Lift finger 1 off the 3rd and it becomes Csus2.
  • Fmaj7 (xx3210) belongs here by sound: it's the IV chord of C with the open top string as its 7th, and its 3-2-1 diagonal sits right next to the C shape.
{{tip: Hearing it in a record}}

The verse of the Beatles' “Something” moves C → Cmaj7 → C7: one note walking down C, B, B♭ inside a chord that otherwise doesn't change. On the open C shape that's finger 1, then nothing, then the little finger on string 3 — exactly the moves above. Chapter 11 calls this a line cliché.

{{/tip}}

The minor families

Minor chords take the same moves. The ♭7 is the most common colour: it softens a minor chord rather than adding tension.

{{chords: Am_open, Am7_open, Ammaj7_open, Am6_open, Em_open, Em7_open, Em7_open2, Emadd9_open, Em6_open, Dm_open, Dm7_open, Dm6_open}}
  • Am7: lift finger 3; the string-3 root falls to open G.
  • Am → Am(maj7) → Am7 → Am6: a line walking down A, G♯, G, F♯. The first three steps are the string-3 note (fret 2, 1, open); the F♯ can't go lower on that string, so Am6 puts it on the top string. That's the minor line cliché.
  • Em7: drop the string-4 root to open D, or add D on string 2 at fret 3 — the version that shares fingers with the rock G.
  • Em(add9): the string-4 root rises to F♯ at fret 4.
  • Dm7: the string-2 root drops to C; one finger flattens across strings 2 and 1.

Why this matters for changing chords

Because colour chords are one finger away from shapes your hand already knows, changing between them costs almost nothing. That makes them the best possible first step in vocabulary: new sounds with no new motor learning. The new shapes — the ones that cost real practice — start in Chapter 8. Chapter 7 is about how to make those cheaper.

{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • For each of A, D, E, G and C, play: chord → sus4 → chord → sus2 → chord. One strum each.
  • Play G → G7 → Gmaj7 → G6 and then C → Cmaj7 → C7, moving one finger at a time.
  • Play Am → Am(maj7) → Am7 → Am6, two beats each, and listen to the line walking down.
  • Swap every chord in a song you know for its maj7 or add9. Keep the ones you like.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

In open D, which string carries the 3rd, and what do you get by moving it up a whole step?

String 1 (F♯). Up a whole step is G, the 4th: Dsus4.

Open G's top string plays the root. What three chords do you get by lowering it to fret 2, fret 1, and open?

Gmaj7 (F♯), G7 (F), G6 (E).

Why does moving a doubled root keep the chord's name, while moving the 3rd changes it?

The chord's identity comes from its root and 3rd. With another root still sounding, changing a spare root just adds a note (a 7, 6 or 9). The 3rd is usually the only copy, so moving it removes major/minor altogether.

What's the one-note difference between Cadd9 (x32033) and Csus2 (x30033)?

The E on string 4 (the 3rd). Cadd9 keeps it; Csus2 drops it, so the open D replaces it.
