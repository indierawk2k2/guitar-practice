--- title: The raw material order: 20 summary: Twelve notes, half and whole steps, the major scale formula, and what intervals look like on the neck. level: 1 ---

Every chord name, scale and key on this site is built from one small idea: there are twelve notes, and everything else is a way of choosing some of them and measuring the distances between them. This chapter gives you the ruler.

Twelve notes, one fret apart

Western music uses twelve notes, then repeats them an octave higher ({{link:learn:the-vocabulary | the previous chapter}} shows where the twelve come from). On the guitar each fret is one of those steps. Seven of the notes have letter names, A to G. The other five sit between them and borrow a neighbour's name with ♯ (sharp, one fret up) or ♭ (flat, one fret down):

A · A♯/B♭ · B · C · C♯/D♭ · D · D♯/E♭ · E · F · F♯/G♭ · G · G♯/A♭ · A

Two pairs have no note between them: B–C and E–F. Everything else is two frets apart. That one irregularity is why the piano has groups of two and three black keys, and it is the only fact you need to name any note on the neck: count up from an open string.

{{neck: root=E formula="1" frets=0-12 labels=notes title="E on every string, nut to fret 12"}} {{why: Why two names for one note?}}

A♯ and B♭ are the same fret but not the same word. The name depends on the key: in F major the fourth note is B♭ (because the scale already has an A, and each letter is used once); in B major the same sound is A♯. Chapter 7 shows why keys work that way. For now, either name is fine when you're just finding a fret.

{{/why}}

Half steps and whole steps

One fret is a half step (semitone). Two frets is a whole step (tone). These are the only two measurements you need for scales.

The major scale formula

Start on any note and take these steps: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half (W W H W W W H). You get the major scale — do re mi fa sol la ti do. From C you land only on natural notes:

Step to nextWWHWWWH
G majorGABCDEF♯G

Start on G and the formula forces one sharp: E to the next note must be a whole step, so it's F♯, not F. That one sharp is the key signature of G. Each key is just this formula started somewhere else.

{{try: The scale on one string}}

Play C major up the A string alone: frets 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15. Say W W H W W W H as you move. Then do the same starting at fret 10 on the low E string (D major). One string makes the formula physical: you can see the two half steps.

{{/try}}

Intervals: the distance between two notes

An interval is the distance between two notes, named by counting letters (C up to E counts C-D-E: a third) and then sized by the number of half steps. Chord names are built from these words, so it's worth learning the twelve:

The numbers in brackets are the same notes an octave up: a 2nd plus an octave is a 9th, a 4th plus an octave is an 11th, a 6th plus an octave is a 13th. That's where the big chord numbers come from (Chapter 5).

{{why: Why “perfect”, “major” and “minor”?}}

Unisons, 4ths, 5ths and octaves come in one size within the major scale, so they are called perfect. 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths come in two sizes: major (Latin “greater”) and minor (“lesser”, one half step smaller). That's all the words mean — sizes of a distance. A major chord is named after its major 3rd. The full story, with the counting and the history, is in {{link:learn:the-vocabulary | the previous chapter}}.

{{/why}}

Interval shapes on the neck

Standard tuning is a stack of 4ths — E to A, A to D, D to G, B to E — with one exception: G to B is a major 3rd. That gives every interval a fixed shape between neighbouring strings, plus a second version wherever the shape crosses from the G string to the B string. Learn the shapes, not the frets: they work in every key.

{{scale: interval_P4_C}}

4th: same fret, next string up. Across G–B: one fret higher.

{{scale: interval_P5_C}}

5th: next string up, two frets higher — the power-chord shape. Across G–B: three frets higher.

{{scale: interval_M3_C}}

Major 3rd: next string up, one fret lower. Across G–B: same fret.

{{scale: interval_m3_C}}

Minor 3rd: next string up, two frets lower. Across G–B: one fret lower.

Octave shapes

The same note an octave higher is two strings up and two frets higher (string 6 to 4, 5 to 3). If the jump lands on or crosses the B string, it's three frets higher (4 to 2, 3 to 1). And the two E strings are two octaves apart, so string 6 and string 1 always share a note name at the same fret.

{{neck: root=C formula="1" frets=0-15 labels=notes title="Every C from the nut to fret 15"}} {{tip: The G–B kink}}

Every shape on the guitar — chords, scales, arpeggios — has a dent where it crosses from string 3 to string 2. When a shape you know from the low strings looks “wrong” on the top strings, it's almost always this: everything on strings 2 and 1 moves up one fret.

{{/tip}} {{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Play C major on one string, saying W W H W W W H.
  • Pick any fretted note and play its 4th, 5th, major 3rd and octave on the next strings up. Repeat starting on string 3 to feel the G–B kink.
  • Find every C from the nut to fret 15 using only octave shapes. Then every G.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

Which two pairs of natural notes are only a half step apart?

B–C and E–F.

What is the step pattern of the major scale?

W W H W W W H.

How many half steps in a minor 3rd? A perfect 5th? A minor 7th?

3, 7 and 10.

You play C on string 3, fret 5. Where is the C an octave up?

String 1, fret 8 — two strings up and three frets higher, because the jump crosses the G–B kink.
