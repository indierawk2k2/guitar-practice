--- title: The fretboard map order: 90 summary: Note names on strings 6 and 5, octave shapes, the five CAGED shapes as one continuous system, and how a key lies across the whole neck. level: 2 ---

Most guitarists know the neck as a set of islands: open chords at the nut, a barre shape or two, a pentatonic box at fret 5. This chapter joins the islands. Three tools do it: the note names on the bottom two strings, octave shapes, and CAGED.

Strings 6 and 5: learn these cold

Every movable chord and scale is located by its root on string 6 or 5. Learn the natural notes first; the sharps and flats fall in the gaps.

Fret0123456789101112

String 5AA♯/B♭BCC♯/D♭DD♯/E♭EFF♯/G♭GG♯/A♭A

Hooks that make it stick:

  • The inlay frets on string 6 are G (3), A (5), B (7), and at 9 a C♯ you can ignore; 12 is E again.
  • String 5 at the same inlays is C (3), D (5), E (7) and A (12); G is at 10, one past the ninth-fret dot.
  • The fifth-fret rule you use for tuning: string 6 fret 5 = open string 5. So any note on string 6 is also on string 5, five frets lower.
  • Past 12 everything repeats: fret 15 is fret 3 again.

Octave shapes: one note everywhere

From any note on strings 6 or 5, two octave shapes give you the same note on the other strings (Chapter 2): two strings up and two frets up, or — crossing onto the B string — two strings up and three frets up. Strings 6 and 1 always match fret for fret.

{{neck: root=A formula="1" frets=0-15 labels=notes title="Every A, nut to fret 15"}} {{try: The one-note drill}}

Name a note. Find it on all six strings between the nut and fret 12, low to high, saying the string name out loud. Then a new note. Five notes a day for a week and the neck stops being anonymous.

{{/try}}

CAGED: five shapes, one chord

There are five open major shapes: C, A, G, E and D. Each can be moved up the neck with the index finger playing the nut (Chapter 8 did this for E and A). The useful fact is that, for any one chord, the five moved shapes join end to end along the neck in the order C–A–G–E–D, then start again. Where one shape ends, the next begins, sharing notes at the seam.

Here is C major, every C, E and G on the neck, and then the five shapes that cover it:

{{neck: notes="C E G" frets=0-15 labels=intervals title="C major triad, whole neck"}} {{chords: C_open, C_barre_A3, C_caged_G5, C_barre_E8, C_caged_D10}}
  1. C shape at the nut (open C).
  2. A shape at fret 3 — open A's shape, root on string 5.
  3. G shape spanning frets 5–8 — open G's shape, roots on strings 6, 3 and 1.
  4. E shape at fret 8 — the E-shape barre.
  5. D shape at fret 10 — open D's shape, root on string 4.
  6. Then the C shape again at fret 12, an octave above the open C.

Look at the seams. The A shape's top edge (fret 5 on strings 4–2) is the G shape's barre. The G shape's top edge (fret 8 on strings 6 and 1) is the E shape's barre. Each shape grows out of the last.

{{why: Why the order never changes}}

The shapes are always in C–A–G–E–D order because of the geometry of standard tuning: the top edge of each shape is exactly the “nut” of the next one, for any chord. Only the starting point depends on the chord: start with the shape that shares the chord's name in open position. For A you start at A; for G, at G; and so on round the cycle.

{{/why}}

The same system for A

A major starts with the A shape at the nut and continues G–E–D–C:

{{neck: notes="A C# E" frets=0-15 labels=intervals title="A major triad, whole neck"}} {{chords: A_open, A_caged_G2, A_barre_E5, A_caged_D7, A_caged_C9}}
  1. A shape at the nut.
  2. G shape at frets 2–5.
  3. E shape at fret 5.
  4. D shape at frets 7–10.
  5. C shape at frets 9–12, then the A shape again at fret 12.

Which shapes to actually play

Full G- and C-shape barres are stretches nobody plays all six strings of. Their value is as a map: they tell you where the root, 3rd and 5th are in that region. Use fragments — the top three or four strings of each shape are the triads of Chapter 12, and they're the parts that sound good. The E, A and D shapes are fine as full chords.

Minor chords work the same way. Three minor shapes are practical: the E shape, the A shape and the D shape.

How a key lies across the neck

A key is seven notes, and they cover the neck in one continuous pattern. Here is G major everywhere; the squares are G.

{{scale: G_major_neck}}

Chapter 10 cuts this into five overlapping positions, one per CAGED shape. For chords, the practical point is that in any four- or five-fret region you can find every chord of the key using E-shapes, A-shapes and D-shapes. Around fret 2–5 in G: G (E-shape 3), Am (E-shape 5), Bm (A-shape 2), C (A-shape 3), D (A-shape 5), Em (open or A-shape 7). Around fret 7–10: Bm (E-shape 7), C (E-shape 8), D (E-shape 10), Em (A-shape 7), G (A-shape 10), Am (A-shape 12). The same key, two neighbourhoods.

{{tip: Think in regions, not chords}}

When you learn a song, choose a region of the neck and find all its chords there before you start. You'll change chords by moving fingers, not by jumping the whole hand — the change drills in Chapter 7 get much easier.

{{/tip}} {{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Say the natural notes on string 6, then string 5, while playing them, up to fret 12.
  • Play C major in all five CAGED shapes, low to high, then the same for A.
  • Do the one-note drill for five notes.
  • Play G–Em–C–D twice: once around fret 3, once around fret 7.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

What note is at string 6, fret 8? String 5, fret 10?

C and G.

What is the CAGED order, and which shape comes after the E shape?

C–A–G–E–D, repeating. After E comes D.

For G major, which shape is at the nut and which comes next going up?

The G shape (open G) at the nut, then the E shape (barre at fret 3).

You play an A note on string 5, fret 12. Where's the same pitch on string 6?

Fret 17 (five frets higher on the lower string). An octave lower it's at fret 5.
