The program has six levels, but the point of it is the habits, not the levels. This chapter is the toolkit for after — or alongside — the packets: how to choose what to learn, how to understand it quickly, and how to turn the hard part into a drill.

Levelling up

  • Exit criteria, not the calendar. Move on when every box is ticked. If a level takes six weeks instead of three, the next one will be easier for it.
  • Keep the log honest. The one-minute change counts and top clean tempos are the only objective record you have. Plateaus show up there before you feel them.
  • Plateaus are normal. Progress on a new shape comes in steps, often after a few days of apparently nothing. If a number hasn't moved in two weeks, change the drill (slower, smaller, different pairing) rather than doing more of it.
  • Review, don't repeat. When starting a new level, spend five minutes of each session on the previous level's hardest change. It keeps it from fading.

Picking the next song

The best next song has one new thing in it and everything else familiar. One new chord type, one new change, one new rhythm — not three.

  • Pick songs you want to play. Motivation is a practice tool.
  • Before committing, run the five-minute analysis below. If it turns up more than two changes you can't do, park the song and come back after the next level.
  • A song that's slightly too easy is still worth learning well: clean changes at full tempo, in a different region of the neck, with different voicings.

Analysing any song in five minutes

  1. Find the key. Which chord feels like home? Often the first and last. Check the rest fit that key's seven chords (Chapter 7). If the home chord is minor, try the relative minor.
  2. Number the chords. Write the roman numerals under the chord names. Now you know the song's shape, and you can play it in any key.
  3. Spot the outsider. Any chord that isn't in the key: borrowed from the parallel minor (iv, ♭VII, ♭VI, ♭III), a secondary dominant (a 7 chord or a major chord where the key has a minor), or a passing chord on a moving line (Chapter 12). Name its job.
  4. Find the hard change. Look at each pair of neighbouring chords and ask which one you can't make in time. Usually it's one change, into or out of the outsider or a new shape.
  5. Choose a region. Pick the voicings: open, barre, or triads, all within a few frets if possible (Chapter 10). Look for anchors and guides (Chapter 8) and pick voicings that share fingers.

A worked example: Radiohead, “Creep”

  1. Key: G – B – C – Cm, round and round. G feels like home: key of G.
  2. Numbers: I – III – IV – iv.
  3. Outsiders: B major (III — a secondary dominant of Em, never resolved) and Cm (iv, borrowed from G minor).
  4. Hard change: G to B if B is a barre; C to Cm if you play C open, because Cm can't be.
  5. Region: G as an E-shape at fret 3, B as an A-shape at fret 2, C and Cm as A-shapes at fret 3. Now C → Cm is one finger change (the 3rd drops a fret), and the whole song sits between frets 2 and 5.

Five minutes, and you know what to practise: G → B as a change loop, then the whole cycle on the tempo ladder.

Building your own drills

Every drill in the packets is built from the named drills in Chapter 8. For a new song:

  1. Take the hard change from the analysis.
  2. Anchor hunt on it; if the voicing makes the change awkward, try another voicing first.
  3. Change loop until ten clean in a row, then tempo ladder one chord per bar.
  4. One-minute changes daily; write the number.
  5. Song loop on the two bars around it.

Give the drill a line in your log with today's number. When the number stops moving, change one variable: slower, a different voicing, or practise it at the start of the session instead of the end.

Learning by ear, a little at a time

Every analysis gets faster if you can hear the bass. Find the bass note of each chord on string 6 or 5 by humming it and searching; the bass is usually the root. Once you know the roots and the key, the quality (major or minor) is a two-way guess. A few songs a month this way and the chord charts you find online will start to look wrong to you — which is a good sign.

Check yourself

What are the five steps of the five-minute analysis?

Find the key; number the chords; spot the outsider; find the hard change; choose a region and voicings.

What makes a good next song?

One new thing — one chord type, change or rhythm — with everything else familiar, and a song you actually want to play.

Your one-minute number hasn't moved in two weeks. What do you change?

The drill, not the amount: go slower, change the voicing or the pairing, or move it to the start of the session.
