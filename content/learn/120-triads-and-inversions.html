A full six-string chord is a lot of sound. Next to a bass player, a keyboard or a second guitar it's often too much — the low strings fight the bass and the whole thing turns to mud. The answer most good rhythm players use is the triad: just the three notes of the chord, on three adjacent strings. Small shapes, easy to move, and every one of them is a fragment of a CAGED shape you already know.

Three notes, three orders

A triad has three notes, so on three adjacent strings it can be stacked three ways, depending on which note is lowest:

  • Root position: R – 3 – 5 from the lowest string up.
  • First inversion: 3 – 5 – R. Written G/B.
  • Second inversion: 5 – R – 3. Written G/D.

All three are the same chord. Moving up the neck you meet them in a fixed cycle — root, 1st, 2nd, root… — because each shape starts on the middle note of the one below it. Three shapes per string set cover the whole neck.

G major on strings 1-2-3

The top three strings, from low to high on the neck:

{{chords: G_tri123_inv1, G_tri123_inv2, G_tri123_root}}
  • G/B (fret 3): the top of the E-shape barre at fret 3.
  • G/D (frets 7–8): the shape of the top of an open D chord — and of an open C chord.
  • G (frets 10–12): the top of the A-shape barre at fret 10 (open A's top three strings, moved up).

Then G/B again at fret 15. Each shape is the top three strings of a CAGED shape: E, D (or C) and A. That's why Chapter 10 said the big shapes are maps — these are the parts you actually play.

Slash chords are inversions (mostly)

When the note after the slash is in the chord, a slash chord is just an inversion: C/E, G/B, D/F♯. When it isn't — C/B♭, G/F♯ — it's a triad over a foreign bass, which creates a new chord (C7 and Gmaj7 respectively) but is usually heard as a line passing through. Either way, it's an instruction about the bass, which is why slash chords come in groups that spell out a moving line.

Walking a bass line under a held chord

A powerful trick on solo guitar: hold the top of a chord with the fingers and walk the bass beneath it, step by step. Hold the top three strings of open D (xxx232) and play these bass notes on strings 4 and 5:

{{grid: D | D/C# | D/B | D/A ; title="D – D/C♯ – D/B – D/A: bass D, C♯, B, A"}}
  • D: open string 4.
  • D/C♯: string 5, fret 4 — sounds as Dmaj7.
  • D/B: string 5, fret 2 — sounds as Bm7.
  • D/A: open string 5.

The top never moves; the harmony still travels. The same idea in reverse — a bass walking up between chords — is the country walk-up: G, then A and B on string 5, landing on C.

Harmonising the scale in triads

Here is the payoff. Take any triad shape on one string set and move each of its three notes up to the next note of the scale. You get the next chord of the key, in the same inversion. Repeat and you walk through all seven chords of the key on one string set.

G major on strings 3-2-1, starting from G/B at fret 3 (frets listed string 3, 2, 1):

FretsNotesChordNumeral
4 3 3B D GG/BI
5 5 5C E AAm/Cii
7 7 7D F♯ BBm/Diii
9 8 8E G CC/EIV
11 10 10F♯ A DD/F♯V
12 12 12G B EEm/Gvi
14 13 14A C F♯F♯°/Avii°
16 15 15B D GG/BI

Every chord here is in first inversion, so the shapes repeat: the three major chords (I, IV, V) share one shape, the three minors (ii, iii, vi) another, and vii° is the odd one out. Play it up and down slowly and you'll hear the key as a ladder of chords.

{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Play G major triads on strings 1-2-3 up the neck — G/B, G/D, G, G/B — then the minor versions.
  • Play a song's chords using only 1-2-3 triads, staying within five frets. Choose the nearest inversion each time.
  • Walk the bass D – C♯ – B – A under the held top of a D chord.
  • Harmonise G major up strings 3-2-1 using the table, naming each numeral.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

Which note is lowest in a second-inversion triad?

The 5th. A second-inversion G is G/D.

What turns G/B (xxx433) into Gm/B♭?

Lowering the 3rd: the B on string 3, fret 4 drops to B♭ at fret 3, giving xxx333.

Hold D's top three strings and play C♯ in the bass. What chord does it sound like?

Dmaj7 (D F♯ A C♯), in third inversion — written D/C♯.

Harmonising a major scale in triads, which three chords share one shape?

The three major chords, I, IV and V (and the three minors, ii, iii and vi, share another).
