--- title: The vocabulary, and how it connects order: 15 summary: From a vibrating string to the chords of a key — overtones, why simple ratios sound smooth, why there are twelve notes, where the major scale comes from, and every chord word placed where it belongs. level: 1 ---

You have played major and minor chords for years by ear and by shape. The words around them — major, minor, diminished, augmented, sus, seventh — look like a pile of unrelated labels. They are one family tree, and its root is physical: a string vibrating between two fixed points. This chapter is the map: one chain of reasoning from that string to the chords of a key, with every word placed where it belongs. The first half is the physics and why it sounds the way it does; the second half is the vocabulary built on top. The next three chapters are the detailed treatments; this one points at them as it goes.

Where the notes come from

{{tip: The chain in one paragraph}}

A string pinned at both ends can only vibrate in whole numbers of equal pieces, so it sounds at f, 2f, 3f, 4f… all at once: every note is a stack of overtones. The ratios between neighbouring overtones — 2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 6:5 — are the octave, 5th, 4th, major 3rd and minor 3rd. Notes in a simple ratio share overtones and have few near-misses to beat against each other, so they sound smooth. Stacking 5ths comes back almost exactly to the start after twelve steps: twelve notes. “Almost” means the frets have to compromise, so they split the octave into twelve equal steps. Seven of the twelve make the major scale.

{{/tip}}

A pinned string vibrates in whole numbers of pieces

A guitar string is held still at two points, the nut (or your fretting finger) and the saddle. A ripple running along it reflects at each end and runs back, and the only shapes that survive the back-and-forth are the ones that fit the length exactly, with a still point at each end. These are standing waves: the whole string swinging as one arch; two arches flapping in opposite directions with a still point in the middle; three arches; four; and so on. Any other shape would need an end to move. The still points are nodes.

On the same string at the same tension, a ripple travels at a fixed speed, so a shorter vibrating piece swings proportionally faster: half the length, twice the frequency. (That's why the 12th fret, which halves the string, gives the octave.) An arch a third of the string long swings three times as fast. So if the one-arch shape vibrates at frequency f, the shape with n arches vibrates at exactly n × f. The available frequencies are f, 2f, 3f, 4f, 5f… and nothing in between.

One note is a stack

A pick doesn't set up any one of these shapes. It pulls the string into a sharp bend and lets go, and that bend is a mixture of all of them (any shape that fits between the pinned ends can be built by adding standing waves together). So a plucked string sounds all those frequencies at once, the lower ones usually loudest, and your ear fuses the stack into one note with the pitch of f.

The words, once. The lowest component, f, is the fundamental. The whole set are the harmonics or partials; the ones above the fundamental are overtones. Careful with the numbering: the 2nd harmonic is 2f, the first overtone. This chapter numbers by the multiple. The mix of loudnesses is the timbre — why picking near the bridge sounds bright (a sharp bend, rich in high harmonics) and picking over the 12th fret sounds round (a pick at the middle can't start any shape that has a node there, so the 2nd, 4th, 6th… harmonics drop out).

None of this is special to guitars. The air in a flute or trumpet is a standing wave between two ends with the same whole-number series (a clarinet, closed at one end, favours the odd ones), and the voice, like any steadily repeating vibration, is a stack at f, 2f, 3f… too. So what follows is about pitched sound in general. (Real strings are slightly stiff, which nudges the highest harmonics sharp; on a guitar it is small enough to ignore.)

On the low E, rest a fingertip on the string right over the fret wire — touching, not pressing — pick near the bridge, and lift. The touch forces a node there: every shape that needs that point to move stops, and you hear the lowest shape that has a node there.

  • Fret 7, a third of the way (or fret 19): 3f, B, an octave and a 5th up.
  • Fret 5, a quarter: 4f, E two octaves up.
  • Just on the nut side of fret 4 (about “fret 3.9”), a fifth: 5f, G♯, two octaves and a major 3rd up.
  • Just past fret 3 (about “fret 3.2”), a sixth: 6f, B again. Faint.

With the open string first: E, E, B, E, G♯, B — the notes of an E major chord, out of one string.

Intervals are the ratios between overtones

Compare each rung of the stack with the next: 2f to f is 2:1, 3f to 2f is 3:2, and so on. Nobody chose the rows below. They are simply the first rungs, and so the smallest whole-number ratios there are. Each is an interval you already know by ear; every note you've heard carried them inside it. (The names are explained once there's a scale to count on.)

They are special twice over. The smaller the numbers, the more overtones the two notes share (next section). And the low harmonics are the loudest in any real note, so these are the relationships the ear is fed most of. Higher rungs shrink and roughen: 9:8 is a whole step, 16:15 a half step. The 7th harmonic is the odd one: 7:4 is roughly a minor 7th but about a third of a half step flat of the fret, and Western tuning doesn't use it directly. Rungs that aren't neighbours give the rest: 5f to 3f is 5:3, a major 6th; 8f to 5f is 8:5, a minor 6th. Every interval is in the stack somewhere; the table is the top of the list.

Two things follow. First, harmonics 4, 5 and 6 — E, G♯, B — are root, major 3rd and 5th: a major triad, in the ratio 4:5:6. Every note you play already contains a faint major chord. That is where chords come from.

Second, the frets give the same ratios upside down: frequency rises as length falls, so a ratio of lengths is the inverse of the ratio of frequencies. Fret 12 leaves half the string sounding, 2:1, the octave. Fret 7 leaves two-thirds, 3:2, the 5th. Fret 5 leaves three-quarters, 4:3, the 4th. (Very nearly — the reason for “nearly” is coming.)

Drawn to scale, every interval is a slice of the string, and its pitch is that fraction turned over: two-thirds of the string sounds three-halves the frequency.

{{try: Measure it}}

Measure your guitar from the nut to the saddle: the scale length, about 648 mm (25.5 in) on a Fender-type, 628 mm (24.75 in) on a Gibson-type. The 12th fret is half way. The 7th is a third of the way from the nut, leaving 2/3; the 5th a quarter of the way, leaving 3/4.

Why simple ratios sound smooth

Two physical reasons, pointing the same way.

Shared overtones. Play A at 110 Hz and the E above at 165 Hz, a 3:2 5th. The A's harmonics are 110, 220, 330, 440, 550, 660…; the E's are 165, 330, 495, 660… Every 3rd harmonic of the lower note is exactly every 2nd of the upper, and together the two stacks look like part of the stack of one note an octave below (55 Hz). The ear partly hears them as one thing: they fuse. The octave is the extreme case — every harmonic of the upper note is already one of the lower's — which is why it sounds like the “same” note and why note names repeat.

No near-misses. Two tones close in frequency but not equal drift in and out of step, so the sound swells and fades at a rate equal to their difference: beats, the wah-wah-wah you hear while tuning two strings to the same note, slowing as they converge. A few beats a second is a wobble; tens a second blur into a buzzy roughness. Far enough apart — beyond the ear's critical band, about a minor 3rd in the middle of the range and wider in the bass — two tones stop interfering. Helmholtz argued in the 1860s that roughness between partials is what dissonance is; Plomp and Levelt measured how it depends on the gap in 1965.

In a simple ratio, partials either coincide or sit well apart. In a complicated one like the half step (16:15), nothing coincides and the low partials are all near-misses — 110 against 117, 220 against 235, 330 against 352, beating 7, 15, 22 times a second and up. That is the sound of a half step: rough. (It is also why a 3rd played low in the bass goes muddy: down there even a 3rd puts partials inside each other's critical band.)

Physics, then culture

That explains why simple ratios are smooth. Whether smooth sounds good is partly learned. McDermott and colleagues (Nature, 2016) played chords to the Tsimane' of the Bolivian Amazon, who have had little exposure to Western music. They disliked rough, beating sounds as Westerners do, yet rated consonant and dissonant chords about equally pleasant, where American listeners clearly preferred consonance; a later study (McPherson and colleagues, 2020) found the Tsimane' still hear consonant intervals as more fused. The difference is in every ear; the liking is largely learned from the music around you. How much consonance owes to roughness and how much to harmonicity (how much the combined stack looks like one note's) is still debated, but both favour the same simple ratios.

And dissonant doesn't mean bad. Simple ratios give consonance, the stable sound; complex ones give dissonance, tension, a sense that something ought to move. Tension moving to stability is resolution. Music needs both, and that pull drives everything at the end of this chapter.

Why twelve notes

The octave matches so completely that it counts as the same note, so the question is how to fill one octave. Take the simplest new interval, the 5th, and stack it: C, up a 5th to G, up a 5th to D, and so on, dropping a note by an octave whenever it passes the C above. The chain runs C G D A E B F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ — and the twelfth step lands on B♯, very nearly C again.

In numbers: twelve 5ths multiply the frequency by 1.5¹² ≈ 129.75; seven octaves by 2⁷ = 128. The chain overshoots by about 1.36%. Small differences are measured in cents, hundredths of a half step (1,200 to the octave): the overshoot is about 23.5 cents, a quarter of a fret, and it is called the Pythagorean comma.

Why twelve? It is where the chain first nearly closes with steps of a usable size. It has two earlier resting points: 5 notes (C G D A E, a pentatonic scale) and 7 (F C G D A E B). At each, the notes divide the octave into steps of just two sizes, which helps them hang together as scales, but the next 5th would land about a half step from a note already there: not closed. After 12 the miss is down to the comma; the next count that does clearly better is 53 notes to the octave, far too many to play. The piano keeps the history: seven neighbouring 5ths, F to B, are the white keys; the next five, F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯, are the black keys — a pentatonic scale of their own. The same chain, as the circle of fifths, orders the keys: see {{link:learn:keys-and-numbers}}.

The compromise: equal temperament

The comma means the ratios can't all be pure at once: with every 5th exactly 3:2, the circle doesn't close. The 3rds add a second conflict. Four 5ths up from C reach E; dropped two octaves, that E ought to be a pure major 3rd, 5:4, but (3/2)⁴ = 81/16 is a little more than 80/16. The gap, 81:80 or about 21.5 cents, is the syntonic comma. Tuning to pure ratios, just intonation, can make one key perfect, but other keys on the same instrument then go audibly out.

The fix on every fretted instrument and piano is equal temperament: twelve identical steps per octave, each multiplying the frequency by the twelfth root of 2 ≈ 1.05946, so twelve make exactly 2. That's how frets are placed. Each fret shortens the remaining string by the same proportion, about 5.6%, and because that's 5.6% of an ever-shorter length, the frets crowd together up the neck. In return, every key is exactly like every other and a barre shape sounds the same at every fret — for an instrument whose shapes slide all over the neck, the only practical choice.

On a 648 mm string, frets 7 and 5 land within about half a millimetre of the pure 5th and 4th; the frets for the 3rds miss by four or five.

What it costs on your guitar

  • The fret-4 harmonic. The low-E harmonic just short of fret 4 is a pure G♯ (412.0 Hz). The high E string fretted at 4 is the same G♯, tempered (415.3 Hz). Ring them together and they beat about three times a second: the harmonic is 13.7 cents flat. The fret-7 harmonic against the G string at fret 4 (both B) is only 2 cents off: one slow beat every few seconds.
  • The 3rd in open chords. In open G the 3rd is the open B string; in open E, the G string at fret 1. Those are the notes that sound faintly sour on an otherwise in-tune guitar, and players who nudge that string flat to sweeten one chord put the next one out. That's the 13.7 cents, not your ear.
  • Power chords under distortion. Distortion adds new partials to every note, including sums and differences of the two notes' frequencies. With a nearly pure 5th the new partials nearly coincide; with a tempered 3rd they scatter into near-misses, and a full chord turns to mud. Root and 5th alone, the power chord, stays clean.

The ruler: the major scale

Seven notes out of twelve

Most music leans on seven of the twelve notes at a time, and the most common seven come straight out of what came before, by two routes that agree.

From the chain of 5ths. Take seven neighbouring links, F C G D A E B. Folded into one octave and read from C they are C D E F G A B, and the gaps between them come in only two sizes, whole and half steps, in the order W W H W W W H: the major scale, do re mi. (The same seven notes read from another letter are the modes, in {{link:learn:modes-as-colours}}.)

From the major triad. Every note carries a 4:5:6 major triad. Put one on C (C E G), one on the 5th above (G B D) and one on the 5th below (F A C): together, exactly C D E F G A B. A home note and its two closest relatives — a 5th, the simplest interval after the octave, either side — each with its own major chord — that is the major scale, and it is why I, IV and V are the three major chords of every major key.

The routes agree because four 5ths up from C land within 21.5 cents of the pure 3rd E. In pure ratios they differ slightly — the triad route has whole steps of 9:8 and 10:9 and half steps of 16:15, which is where those ratios come from — and on equal-tempered frets they are identical. So W W H W W W H isn't a rule to memorise: it's what is left when you pick these seven notes.

Measuring with it

Numbered from its first note, the root, the major scale is the ruler every other term is measured against: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7, then 8, which is 1 again an octave up.

This is the most useful single fact in the vocabulary: every number in a chord or scale name means “compared with the major scale built on this root”. ♭3 is one fret below the major scale's 3rd; ♯5 is one fret above its 5th; 7 is the major scale's own 7th. Whatever key the song is in, the ruler is laid down from the root of the thing being named: the ♭3 of A minor is C, because the A major scale has C♯ there.

Notes that belong to the key in play are diatonic (“through the key”); notes from outside it are chromatic. The note a whole key comes home to has its own name: the tonic.

One set of words, three jobs

Here is the main source of confusion. Major, minor, diminished and augmented are used at three levels: to size an interval, to name a chord, and to name a key, and “C minor” can mean a chord or a key depending on the sentence. But at every level they are size words: an interval is the bigger or smaller of its two sizes, and a chord or key is named after the size of the 3rd above its root or home note.

Intervals

The number counts letters. An interval's number counts scale notes, the starting note included: C D E F G is five notes, so C up to G is a 5th; C–E is a 3rd; and octave is Latin for “eighth”. It says how many letter names the interval spans, not its size. Counting both ends is why the arithmetic looks wrong: a 3rd on a 3rd makes a 5th, not a 6th (in C–E–G the E is counted twice), and a 5th plus a 4th makes an octave, 5 + 4 = 8, not 9. The names are far older than any idea of frequency; the Greeks called the 5th diapente, “through five”, the 4th diatessaron, “through four”, and the octave diapason, “through all”.

The word says how many sizes. Measure every interval of each number inside the major scale — every 3rd, C–E, D–F, E–G… — in half steps:

4ths, 5ths and octaves come in one size, bar the one tritone (F up to B, or B up to F). The rest come in two sizes a half step apart, so they needed two names and got the plainest: major and minor are Latin for “greater” and “lesser”. A major 3rd is just the big 3rd. The one-size intervals are perfect: medieval theorists counted the unison, octave and 5th, and usually the 4th, as the perfect consonances. They had imperfect consonances too, but those were the 3rds and 6ths, not some lesser kind of 5th. So there is no major 5th: a 5th a half step off is diminished (smaller) or augmented (larger), words that also take a minor or major interval a further half step. Read from its root, the ruler gives only perfect and major intervals.

The physics lines up. The perfect intervals are the ratios built from 2 and 3 alone: 2:1, 3:2, 4:3. The consonant two-size intervals, the 3rds and 6ths, are the ones that need the 5th harmonic — 5:4 and 6:5, 5:3 and 8:5 — and they come in pairs. Turn an interval upside down, C up to G becoming G up to C, and the numbers add to 9 (the shared note again): a 5th becomes a 4th, a 3rd a 6th. A perfect interval stays perfect; major flips to minor, so C–E, a major 3rd, turned over is E–C, a minor 6th. That is the inversion of an interval.

That odd one out is six half steps, exactly half an octave: the tritone, three whole steps. Spelled C to F♯ it's an augmented 4th; C to G♭, a diminished 5th. Same fret, two names: that's what enharmonic means. In ratio terms the tritone is about as far from simple as an interval gets. Between B and F in the pure-ratio C major scale above it works out at 64:45, and the only small-number ratio anywhere near it is 7:5, which leans on the 7th harmonic that our tuning leaves out. It is the most restless sound in the system. Hold on to it.

Chords

A chord is three or more notes at once, and the overtone series already showed the model: harmonics 4, 5 and 6, root, major 3rd and 5th. As two steps, 4 to 5 is a major 3rd (5:4) and 5 to 6 a minor 3rd (6:5), and together they make the 5th: 5/4 × 6/5 = 3/2. That is the only way to split a 5th into two consonant intervals — the other split, a 4th plus a whole step (4:3 × 9:8), has a dissonant step in it — and it's why chords are built by stacking 3rds: start on the root, skip a scale note, take the next. Root, 3rd, 5th: a triad. A 3rd is either major (4 half steps) or minor (3), so two stacked 3rds allow exactly four combinations — and those are the four triads:

Now the names explain themselves. When the two 3rds differ, the outer interval comes out a perfect 5th, 3:2, and the chord is named after its bottom 3rd, the one measured from the root: major or minor. When they match, the 5th is pushed off 3:2 — two pure minor 3rds make 36:25, two major 3rds 25:16, neither near a simple ratio — and the chord is named after that: two minor 3rds squeeze the 5th to diminished, two major 3rds stretch it to augmented.

The minor triad is the same two 3rds in the other order, minor below and major above: in whole numbers 10:12:15 (12:10 = 6:5, 15:12 = 5:4, 15:10 = 3:2). Same intervals, but much higher in the overtone series than 4:5:6, and not built into a single note the way the major triad is. Whether that is why minor sounds darker is an old idea and still debated; the link between minor and sadness is at least partly learned.

So major versus minor is decided by one note, the 3rd, and your hands already know it. Lift finger 1 off open E and the G♯ on string 3 drops to open G: that finger was the 3rd. In open A the 3rd is the C♯ on string 2; lower it one fret and you have Am. Your open chords, dissected string by string, are in {{link:learn:how-chords-are-built}}.

The two rarer triads are worth a strum each. Diminished sounds pinched and anxious; augmented sounds lifted off the ground, like a question. The augmented triad is also perfectly symmetrical: one more major 3rd on top lands on the octave (4 + 4 + 4 = 12), so it splits the octave into three equal parts, and C+, E+ and G♯+ are the same three sounds, spelled differently. Caug here is open C with its G raised to G♯; Bdim is the triad that occurs naturally in C major.

Keys

A key is a tonic plus a scale, and it's called major or minor by the same test as a chord: the 3rd above the home note. The major scale has a major 3rd, 6th and 7th above its tonic. The natural minor scale is 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7, the ruler with those three made minor; the perfect 4th and 5th (and the 2nd) are the same in both. Play the C major scale starting from its 6th note, A, and you get exactly A natural minor: the same seven notes with a different home. Keys paired like this are relative major and minor.

At every level, then, major and minor only ever meant greater and lesser. The bright and dark feelings are real but were attached to the words afterwards, and, as with the minor triad, are partly learned.

Sus: suspended, not sustained

“Sustained” is the natural guess, and plenty of players make it. The word is suspended, and it describes something that happens over time. In older music a note from one chord was often held — suspended — while the harmony changed under it, clashed with the new chord, then stepped down into it. Play F, then C with the F still sounding on top: the F is a 4th above C, sitting where C's 3rd should be. It rubs until it falls a half step to E, and the chord settles. That's a 4–3 suspension, and a chord frozen at the moment of the rub is a sus4: the 5th split into a 4th and a whole step instead of two 3rds.

Sus2 puts the 2nd in place of the 3rd instead, and usually wants to rise to it. Either way the 3rd is gone — and since the 3rd is the only thing that decides major or minor, a sus chord is neither. That is its open, hovering sound: it hasn't committed. Modern songs often let it hang without resolving at all; the name just remembers where it came from.

{{chords: Asus2_open, A_open, Asus4_open}}

On open A the 3rd is on string 2: open B gives sus2, fret 3 gives sus4, fret 2 is home. Radiohead's {{link:song:street-spirit | “Street Spirit”}} is built on the same move over A minor: the B-string note steps between the chord and its suspensions inside a constant arpeggio.

A power chord (A5) goes one step further: root and 5th, no 3rd at all. It is neither major nor minor, which is why it fits under almost any riff, and its one interval is the nearly pure 3:2 that survives distortion.

Sevenths: one more 3rd

Stack one more 3rd on a triad and you reach the 7th: four notes, a seventh chord. Same logic as before. The triad underneath is major, minor or diminished; the 7th on top is major (11 half steps, one fret below the octave) or minor (10, two frets below). Two rules make every symbol readable: an “m” straight after the letter only ever touches the 3rd; “maj” only ever touches the 7th. That is how Am(maj7) has a minor 3rd and a major 7th at once. With a minor 7th on a diminished triad, only the triad is diminished — half-diminished, written m7♭5 because that is literally what it is. Shrink the 7th one more half step to a diminished 7th — 9 half steps, the same fret as the 6th but spelled as a 7th because the chord is a stack of 3rds — and every 3rd in it is minor. One more minor 3rd returns to the octave (3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12), so the dim7, like the augmented triad, is symmetrical: it cuts the octave into four equal parts.

{{why: Why a plain “7” means the flat one}}

Build a seventh chord on the 5th note of a major key, using only the key's notes. In C that's G B D F, and F is a minor 7th above G. A major triad with a minor 7th appears naturally on the 5th degree — the dominant — of every major key, and it was so common for so long that it got the shortest name: G7. The chord with a major 7th was rarer in older music, so it got the extra label, maj7. That's why the plain 7 is called a dominant 7th wherever it turns up, even on a chord that isn't the dominant of anything.

{{/why}}

The dominant 7th earns its place because it contains a tritone. In G7 the 3rd (B) and the ♭7 (F) are six half steps apart: the restless, complex-ratio interval from above. And each of them sits a half step from a note of the C chord, so B rises to C and F falls to E. Play G7 then C and the tritone collapses into C's root and 3rd — V7 → I, the strongest pull in tonal music. The pull is not all ratio: barbershop singers tune the F of a G7 down to the 7th harmonic, making the chord a smooth 4:5:6:7, and it still wants to go home, because of those two half steps and centuries of habit. The last bar of a twelve-bar blues is usually that V7, pointing back to the top.

The verse of the Beatles' {{link:song:something | “Something”}} walks the same line down from C: C, Cmaj7, C7 — root, major 7th, minor 7th, one fret at a time.

Past the octave: 6, add9, 9, 11, 13

Keep stacking 3rds above the 7th and you run past the octave into the 9th, 11th and 13th. They are just the 2nd, 4th and 6th again, an octave up (2 + 7 = 9, 4 + 7 = 11, 6 + 7 = 13). The convention is that a big number implies the stack beneath it: C9 has the ♭7 as well as the 9. For the colour without the 7th you say so: add9 is the triad plus the 9 and nothing else. (Cadd9 and Csus2 differ by one note: add9 keeps the 3rd.) A 6 chord adds the 6th to the triad, again with no 7th. Collectively these are extensions. On six strings something has to go, and which notes get dropped — along with every other symbol you'll meet — is in {{link:learn:chord-name-decoder}}.

Function: what a chord does in a key

Last link. Build a triad on each of the seven notes of the major scale, using only the scale's own notes. Because the step pattern never changes, neither do the results: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — I ii iii IV V vi vii° in roman numerals, in every major key. The three major ones, I, IV and V, are the three triads the scale was assembled from. Add a 7th to each and V7 is the only dominant 7th in the key.

Those positions have jobs. The tonic chord, I, is home — the scale was assembled around it — stable, somewhere a song can end. The subdominant, IV, is away: it leaves home without urgency. The dominant, V — above all V7, with its tritone — is tension that points back home. ii tends to behave like IV, and vi can stand in for I. Home, away, tension, home: most progressions you've played for years are that sentence in different words. The chords of every guitar key, relative minors and the circle of fifths are in {{link:learn:keys-and-numbers}}.

And the chain closes. A string that can only vibrate in whole numbers of pieces gave the overtones; their ratios gave the intervals and the difference between smooth and rough; stacking 5ths gave twelve notes, and the comma gave the frets their compromise; three major triads a 5th apart gave the major scale, the ruler; stacking 3rds gave triads and sevenths; stacking them on every scale note gave the chords of a key; and the tensions inside those chords — the 3rd that decides, the tritone that pulls — are why a progression feels like it's going somewhere.

Check yourself

Why are a string's overtones at exactly 2, 3, 4… times its frequency, and nothing in between?

Pinned at both ends, the string can only vibrate in shapes with still points at both ends: a whole number of equal arches. An arch 1/n of the string long vibrates n times as fast, because the same string at the same tension carries waves at a fixed speed.

Why twelve notes?

Stack the simplest new interval, the 5th (3:2), folding back into one octave. Twelve 5ths come within 23.5 cents (the Pythagorean comma) of seven octaves, closing the circle almost exactly; fewer don't close, and the next good fit is 53. Equal temperament closes the gap by making every 5th 2 cents narrow.

The low-E harmonic just short of fret 4 and the high E string fretted at 4 are both G♯. Which is lower, and why?

The harmonic, by about 13.7 cents (412.0 Hz against 415.3 Hz, beating about three times a second). The harmonic is the 5th harmonic, a pure 5:4 major 3rd above the octaves; the fret is an equal-tempered major 3rd, which is that much wider.

Why is there no “major 5th”?

Major and minor mean greater and lesser, and only intervals that come in two sizes need them. Inside the major scale every 3rd is 4 or 3 half steps, but every 5th is 7 (bar the one tritone, B–F), so the 5th has one size and is called perfect. A 5th a half step off that is diminished or augmented.

Why does a 3rd plus a 3rd make a 5th, not a 6th?

Interval numbers count letter names including both ends. C–E counts C D E and E–G counts E F G; together, C–G, the E is counted twice, so 3 + 3 − 1 = 5.

In a diminished triad, what exactly is diminished? And in an augmented triad?

The 5th. Two minor 3rds (3 + 3) put the 5th at 6 half steps, a half step under perfect: diminished. Two major 3rds (4 + 4) put it at 8, a half step over: augmented.

What does “sus” stand for, and why is a sus chord neither major nor minor?

Suspended: a note held over from the previous chord in place of the 3rd. With no 3rd, nothing decides major or minor.

Why does G7 pull so hard toward C?

Its 3rd and ♭7, B and F, form a tritone, the most restless interval in the key. Each is a half step from a note of C major: B rises to C, F falls to E.
