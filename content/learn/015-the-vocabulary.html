--- title: The vocabulary, and how it connects order: 15 summary: Major, minor, diminished, augmented, sus, the sevenths — where each word comes from, why the same words work at three levels, and one chain from a vibrating string to the chords of a key. level: 1 ---

You have played major and minor chords for years by ear and by shape. The words around them — major, minor, diminished, augmented, sus, seventh — look like a pile of unrelated labels. They are one family tree. This chapter is the map: one chain of reasoning from a vibrating string to the chords of a key, with every word placed where it belongs. The next three chapters are the detailed treatments; this one points at them as it goes.

Where the notes come from

A plucked string vibrates as a whole and, at the same time, in halves, thirds, quarters, fifths… Each smaller vibration adds a quieter pitch on top, at 2, 3, 4, 5 times the main frequency. These are the overtones, or harmonics, and you have played them for years: touch a string lightly over the 12th fret and you isolate the halves (an octave up); over the 7th, the thirds (an octave and a 5th); over the 5th, the quarters (two octaves); just short of the 4th fret wire, the fifths (two octaves and a major 3rd).

The ratios between neighbouring overtones are the intervals music is built on: 2:1 is the octave, 3:2 the 5th, 4:3 the 4th, 5:4 the major 3rd, 6:5 the minor 3rd. Notes in a simple ratio share many overtones, so they blend: consonance, the stable sound. Notes in a complicated ratio have overtones that rub: dissonance, tension, a sense that something ought to move. Tension moving to stability is resolution, and it drives everything at the end of this chapter.

The octave blends so completely that we hear its two notes as the “same” note, higher — which is why note names repeat. Stack twelve 5ths and you arrive very nearly back where you started, seven octaves up; those twelve stops, folded into one octave, are the twelve notes. Neighbours are a half step (semitone, one fret) apart; two frets is a whole step (tone). An interval is the distance between two notes, counted in half steps, and everything else here is a choice of notes described by the intervals between them. All twelve intervals, with their shapes on the neck, are in {{link:learn:the-raw-material}}.

The small print: “very nearly” matters. Twelve pure 5ths overshoot seven octaves by about a quarter of a half step, so modern tuning — equal temperament — splits the octave into twelve exactly equal half steps. Every 5th ends up a hair narrow of 3:2 (inaudible), and every major 3rd a little wide of 5:4 (by about a seventh of a half step). The ratios are the physics; the frets are a very good compromise.

The ruler: the major scale

Seven of the twelve notes, chosen by the step pattern W W H W W W H, make the major scale: do re mi. Numbered from its first note, the root, it is the ruler every other term is measured against: 1 2 3 4 5 6 7, then 8, which is 1 again an octave up.

This is the most useful single fact in the vocabulary: every number in a chord or scale name means “compared with the major scale built on this root”. ♭3 is one fret below the major scale's 3rd; ♯5 is one fret above its 5th; 7 is the major scale's own 7th. Whatever key the song is in, the ruler is laid down from the root of the thing being named: the ♭3 of A minor is C, because the A major scale has C♯ there.

Notes that belong to the key in play are diatonic (“through the key”); notes from outside it are chromatic. The note a whole key comes home to has its own name: the tonic.

One set of words, three jobs

Here is the main source of confusion. Major, minor, diminished and augmented are used at three levels: to size an interval, to name a chord, and to name a key. The meanings are related — each level is built out of the one before — but they are not the same thing, and “C minor” can mean a chord or a key depending on the sentence.

Intervals

Measure each note of the major scale from its root. The unison, 4th, 5th and octave are the simple-ratio intervals; they have one standard size and are called perfect. The 2nd, 3rd, 6th and 7th come in two common sizes: the one the major scale gives you is major, a half step smaller is minor. So the ruler reads: perfect unison, major 2nd, major 3rd, perfect 4th, perfect 5th, major 6th, major 7th, perfect octave. Diminished and augmented cover everything else, as in the table. The two families don't mix: a 5th is perfect, diminished or augmented, never “major”.

The odd one out is six half steps, exactly half an octave: the tritone, three whole steps. Spelled C to F♯ it's an augmented 4th; C to G♭, a diminished 5th. Same fret, two names: that's what enharmonic means. The tritone is about as far from a simple ratio as an interval gets — the most restless sound in the system. Hold on to it.

Chords

A chord is three or more notes at once, and the normal way to choose them is to stack 3rds: start on the root, skip a scale note, take the next. Root, 3rd, 5th: a triad. A 3rd is either major (4 half steps) or minor (3), so two stacked 3rds allow exactly four combinations — and those are the four triads:

Now the names explain themselves. When the two 3rds differ, the outer 5th comes out perfect, and the chord is named after its bottom 3rd, the one measured from the root: major or minor. When they match, the 5th is pushed off perfect and the chord is named after that: two minor 3rds squeeze it to diminished, two major 3rds stretch it to augmented.

So major versus minor is decided by one note, the 3rd, and your hands already know it. Lift finger 1 off open E and the G♯ on string 3 drops to open G: that finger was the 3rd. In open A the 3rd is the C♯ on string 2; lower it one fret and you have Am.

The two rarer triads are worth a strum each. Diminished sounds pinched and anxious; augmented sounds lifted off the ground, like a question. The augmented triad is also perfectly symmetrical: one more major 3rd on top lands on the octave (4 + 4 + 4 = 12), so it splits the octave into three equal parts, and C+, E+ and G♯+ are the same three sounds, spelled differently. Caug here is open C with its G raised to G♯; Bdim is the triad that occurs naturally in C major.

{{chords: C_open, Caug_open, Bdim_s5_2}}

Three more words describe how a chord is laid out rather than what it is. Its voicing is which notes go on which strings, in which octaves, and which get doubled. With a note other than the root lowest, it's an inversion: C with E in the bass is C/E, a slash chord. Played one note at a time, it's an arpeggio. Your open chords, dissected string by string, are in {{link:learn:how-chords-are-built}}.

Keys

A key is a tonic plus a scale, and it's called major or minor after the triad built on its tonic: the key of C major comes home to a C major chord, the key of A minor to an A minor chord. The natural minor scale is 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 — the ruler with its 3rd, 6th and 7th lowered — and if you play the C major scale starting from its 6th note, A, that's exactly what you get. A minor uses the same seven notes as C major with a different home; keys paired like this are relative major and minor.

Sus: suspended, not sustained

“Sustained” is the natural guess, and plenty of players make it. The word is suspended, and it describes something that happens over time. In older music a note from one chord was often held — suspended — while the harmony changed under it, clashed with the new chord, then stepped down into it. Play F, then C with the F still sounding on top: the F is a 4th above C, sitting where C's 3rd should be. It rubs until it falls a half step to E, and the chord settles. That's a 4–3 suspension, and a chord frozen at the moment of the rub is a sus4.

Sus2 puts the 2nd in place of the 3rd instead, and usually wants to rise to it. Either way the 3rd is gone — and since the 3rd is the only thing that decides major or minor, a sus chord is neither. That is its open, hovering sound: it hasn't committed. Modern songs often let it hang without resolving at all; the name just remembers where it came from.

{{chords: Asus2_open, A_open, Asus4_open}}

On open A the 3rd is on string 2: open B gives sus2, fret 3 gives sus4, fret 2 is home. Radiohead's {{link:song:street-spirit | “Street Spirit”}} is built on the same move over A minor: the B-string note steps between the chord and its suspensions inside a constant arpeggio.

A power chord (A5) goes one step further: root and 5th, no 3rd at all. It is also neither major nor minor, which is why it fits under almost any riff. Its one interval is the 3:2 of the overtone series, simple enough to stay clean under heavy distortion, where a 3rd's busier ratio turns to mud.

Sevenths: one more 3rd

Stack one more 3rd on a triad and you reach the 7th: four notes, a seventh chord. Same logic as before. The triad underneath is major, minor or diminished; the 7th on top is major (11 half steps, one fret below the octave) or minor (10, two frets below). Two triads times two 7ths give the main four, and the diminished triad adds two more:

Two rules make every symbol readable: an “m” straight after the letter only ever touches the 3rd; “maj” only ever touches the 7th. That is how Am(maj7) has a minor 3rd and a major 7th at once. With a minor 7th on a diminished triad, only the triad is diminished — half-diminished, written m7♭5 because that is literally what it is. Shrink the 7th one more half step to a diminished 7th — 9 half steps, the same fret as the 6th but spelled as a 7th because the chord is a stack of 3rds — and every 3rd in it is minor. One more minor 3rd returns to the octave (3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12), so the dim7, like the augmented triad, is symmetrical: it cuts the octave into four equal parts.

{{why: Why a plain “7” means the flat one}}

Build a seventh chord on the 5th note of a major key, using only the key's notes. In C that's G B D F, and F is a minor 7th above G. A major triad with a minor 7th appears naturally on the 5th degree — the dominant — of every major key, and it was so common for so long that it got the shortest name: G7. The chord with a major 7th was rarer in older music, so it got the extra label, maj7. That's why the plain 7 is called a dominant 7th wherever it turns up, even on a chord that isn't the dominant of anything.

{{/why}}

The dominant 7th earns its place because it contains a tritone. In G7 the 3rd (B) and the ♭7 (F) are six half steps apart: the restless interval. Each of them sits a half step from a note of the C chord, so B rises to C and F falls to E. Play G7 then C and the tritone collapses into C's root and 3rd — V7 → I, the strongest pull in tonal music. The last bar of a twelve-bar blues is usually that V7, pointing back to the top.

{{chords: A_open, Amaj7_open, A7_open, Am7_open}}

Three of the four sevenths on one root, each one finger from a chord you know: the string-3 root of A drops to G♯ for Amaj7 or to open G for A7; in Am the same drop gives Am7. The verse of the Beatles' {{link:song:something | “Something”}} walks the same line down from C: C, Cmaj7, C7 — root, major 7th, minor 7th, one fret at a time.

Past the octave: 6, add9, 9, 11, 13

Keep stacking 3rds above the 7th and you run past the octave into the 9th, 11th and 13th. They are just the 2nd, 4th and 6th again, an octave up (2 + 7 = 9, 4 + 7 = 11, 6 + 7 = 13). The convention is that a big number implies the stack beneath it: C9 has the ♭7 as well as the 9. For the colour without the 7th you say so: add9 is the triad plus the 9 and nothing else. (Cadd9 and Csus2 differ by one note: add9 keeps the 3rd.) A 6 chord adds the 6th to the triad, again with no 7th. Collectively these are extensions. On six strings something has to go, and which notes get dropped — along with every other symbol you'll meet — is in {{link:learn:chord-name-decoder}}.

Function: what a chord does in a key

Last link. Build a triad on each of the seven notes of the major scale, using only the scale's own notes. Because the step pattern never changes, neither do the results: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — I ii iii IV V vi vii° in roman numerals, in every major key. Add a 7th to each and V7 is the only dominant 7th in the key.

Those positions have jobs. The tonic chord, I, is home: stable, somewhere a song can end. The subdominant, IV, is away: it leaves home without urgency. The dominant, V — above all V7, with its tritone — is tension that points back home. ii tends to behave like IV, and vi can stand in for I. Home, away, tension, home: most progressions you've played for years are that sentence in different words. The chords of every guitar key, relative minors and the circle of fifths are in {{link:learn:keys-and-numbers}}.

And the chain closes. Simple ratios gave the notes and intervals; the major scale gave the ruler; stacking 3rds gave triads and sevenths; stacking them on every scale note gave the chords of a key; and the tensions inside those chords — the 3rd that decides, the tritone that pulls — are why a progression feels like it's going somewhere.

Check yourself

Why is a 5th called “perfect” while a 3rd is “major” or “minor”?

4ths and 5ths (with unisons and octaves) have one standard size — the simple-ratio one — so they're called perfect. 3rds, like 2nds, 6ths and 7ths, come in two common sizes: major (what the major scale gives) and minor (a half step smaller).

In a diminished triad, what exactly is diminished? And in an augmented triad?

The 5th. Two minor 3rds (3 + 3) put the 5th at 6 half steps, a half step under perfect: diminished. Two major 3rds (4 + 4) put it at 8, a half step over: augmented.

What does “sus” stand for, and why is a sus chord neither major nor minor?

Suspended: a note held over from the previous chord in place of the 3rd. With no 3rd, nothing decides major or minor.

Why does G7 pull so hard toward C?

Its 3rd and ♭7, B and F, form a tritone. Each is a half step from a note of C major: B rises to C, F falls to E.
