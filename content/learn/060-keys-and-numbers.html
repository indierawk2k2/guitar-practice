--- title: Keys and the number system order: 60 summary: The seven chords of a key, roman numerals, why I–IV–V–vi run pop music, relative minors, and the circle of fifths as a working tool. level: 1 ---

A key is a home note plus the seven notes of its scale. Build a chord on each of those seven notes using only notes from the scale, and you get the seven chords that “belong” to the key. Most songs you love use four or five of them. Number them, and every song becomes a pattern you can play in any key.

Harmonising the scale

Take C major (C D E F G A B) and stack 3rds on each note, using only those seven letters — every other note, as in Chapter 4:

DegreeNotes3rds (bottom, top)Chord
1C E Gmajor, minorC
2D F Aminor, majorDm
3E G Bminor, majorEm
4F A Cmajor, minorF
5G B Dmajor, minorG
6A C Eminor, majorAm
7B D Fminor, minorBdim

The pattern is the same in every major key, because every major key has the same step pattern: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. Written as roman numerals:

I   ii   iii   IV   V   vi   vii°

The guitar keys

These are the keys open-chord guitar lives in. Learn the I, IV, V and vi of each until you can name them without thinking.

Notice what each key costs you. G and C are almost all open chords. D needs one barre (Bm, F♯m). A and E need two or three. That's why so many songs live in G, C, D, A and E, and why the barre chords in Chapter 9 unlock every other key.

The seventh chords of a key

Stack one more 3rd and each chord becomes a four-note chord. Again the pattern is fixed:

Imaj7   ii7   iii7   IVmaj7   V7   vi7   viiø7

KeyImaj7ii7iii7IVmaj7V7vi7viiø7
CCmaj7Dm7Em7Fmaj7G7Am7Bm7♭5

Only one chord in a major key is a dominant 7th: the V7. That's why a 7 chord is such a strong signpost — it points at the chord a 5th below it. When you see E7 in a song in G, something is pointing at Am (Chapter 12).

Why I, IV, V and vi run everything

  • Coverage. I (1 3 5), IV (4 6 1) and V (5 7 2) between them contain all seven notes of the scale. Any melody in the key can be harmonised with three chords.
  • Pull. V contains the 7th degree of the key, a half step below home. In G, D contains F♯, which wants to rise to G. Add the ♭7 (D7 adds C) and the C wants to fall to B, the 3rd of G. V → I is the strongest move in tonal music.
  • The minor partner. vi shares two notes with I (Em and G share G and B). It's a darker version of home, so it can stand in for I.

Four chords, thousands of songs. Rotate the same four and you get other standards: I–vi–IV–V (G Em C D), vi–IV–I–V (Em C G D). Three-chord rock is I–IV–V: the Beatles' “Twist and Shout” is D–G–A, I–IV–V in D.

Relative minor

The vi chord's key uses exactly the same seven notes as the major key. A minor (A B C D E F G) is C major started on A. They are relative keys: same key signature, different home.

In a minor key, renumber from the minor root: i ii° ♭III iv v ♭VI ♭VII. In A minor: Am, B°, C, Dm, Em, F, G. One change is common: the v is often made major (E or E7 instead of Em) to get the strong V → i pull, by raising G to G♯. That raised note is the “harmonic minor”.

R.E.M.'s “Losing My Religion” rocks between Am and Em — i and v in A minor, with the minor v left minor. Swap that Em for E and it becomes a different, more classical sound.

The circle of fifths

Go up a 5th from C and you reach G; G major needs one sharp. Up another 5th: D, two sharps. Each step up a 5th adds one sharp. Going the other way, down a 5th (which is up a 4th), adds one flat. Arranged in a circle:

F♯ major and G♭ major are the same sounds spelled two ways; that's where the two directions meet and close the circle. (F♯ major's sixth sharp is E♯, not F: each letter appears once in a scale.)

{{why: Why sharps go F C G D A E B}}

Each new key is a 5th above the last, and its new sharp is its own 7th degree — a half step below the key's name. G's new sharp is F♯; D's is C♯; A's is G♯. So the sharps also arrive in 5ths: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. Flats arrive in the reverse order: B, E, A, D, G, C, F. Quick rule: in a sharp key, the last sharp is a half step below the key name. In a flat key (from B♭ on), the second-to-last flat is the key name.

{{/why}}

Using the circle

  • Neighbours. A key's neighbours on the circle are its IV (one step anticlockwise) and V (one step clockwise). Neighbouring keys share six of seven notes, which is why modulating to them sounds smooth.
  • Chord chains. Reading anticlockwise, each chord is the V of the next: A → D → G → C. That's the “circle progression” — E7 A7 D7 G7 C is ragtime, and ii–V–I is its last three steps.
  • Transposing. To move a song to another key, move every numeral, not every chord. I–vi–IV–V in G is G Em C D; in D it's D Bm G A.

Finding the key of a song

  • The chord that sounds like the end of a sentence is usually I. The last chord of the song often is.
  • Look for two major chords a whole step apart (C and D): they're probably IV and V, and I is a 4th above the lower one (G).
  • If the home chord is minor and the others fit the same key signature, you're in the relative minor.
  • A chord that doesn't fit is a clue, not a contradiction: Chapter 12.
{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Play the seven chords of G in order, up and back, saying the numerals.
  • Play I–V–vi–IV in G, C, D, A and E. Same pattern each time.
  • Take a song you know in G and play it in D by numbers, without writing the chords down.

Check yourself

What are the I, IV, V and vi chords in D?

D, G, A, Bm.

Which chord in a major key is naturally a dominant 7th?

The V7 (G7 in C, D7 in G).

How many sharps does E major have, and which?

Four: F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯.

What is the relative minor of F, and what key signature do they share?

D minor; one flat, B♭.
