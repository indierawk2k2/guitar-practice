Chapter 7 gave you the seven chords that belong to a key. Plenty of songs you love use an eighth or ninth chord that doesn't — and those outsiders are usually the moment in the song you remember. They're not random. Nearly all of them come from three sources: the parallel minor key, a dominant chord pointing at somewhere in the key, or a bass line moving by step.

Borrowed chords: the parallel minor

G major and G minor share a root but not a key signature. G minor's chords are Gm, A°, B♭, Cm, Dm, E♭, F. Borrowing one of those into a song in G major darkens the moment without leaving home. The four most borrowed, with their numerals in the major key:

ivCmFmGmBittersweet; the IV chord turning sad

The ♭ in ♭VII means the chord's root is a half step below where the key's own VII would be: F instead of F♯.

{{chords: G_open, C_barre_A3, Cm_barre_A3, F_barre_E1}}

IV → iv is the most affecting one-note change in pop. The 3rd of the IV chord drops a half step — in G, the C chord's E becomes E♭ — and E♭ wants to keep falling to D, the 5th of G. In the Beatles' “In My Life” it's the D → Dm (IV → iv in A) late in the verse.

♭VII is everywhere in rock. The coda of “Hey Jude” is F – E♭ – B♭ – F: I – ♭VII – IV – I in F.

{{grid: F | Eb | Bb | F ; title="I – ♭VII – IV – I in F"}}

♭VI – ♭VII – I is the triumphant ending: in C, A♭ – B♭ – C. Every chord is a major chord a whole step from the next.

The major III and the major II

Here's the other kind of outsider: a chord that's in the key, but with its quality flipped from minor to major. Making iii into III, or ii into II, adds a note from outside the key — and that note is always a half step below a note of the next chord, so it pulls forward.

  • III in G is B major instead of Bm: it brings in D♯, which leans up to E. Radiohead's “Creep” is G – B – C – Cm: I – III – IV – iv. One song, two outsiders.
  • II in D is E major instead of Em: it brings in G♯, which leans up to A. The Beatles' “Eight Days a Week” opens D – E – G – D: I – II – IV – I. They used the major II constantly.

Secondary dominants

The V7 chord points at I (Chapter 7). Any chord in the key can be given its own V7, a dominant 7th a 5th above it. These are called secondary dominants and written V7/ii (“five of two”), V7/vi and so on. They're the main source of 7 chords that aren't the V.

iiiEmB7BmF♯7

The giveaway is a major 3rd that isn't in the key. E7 in C contains G♯, a half step below A: it has to go to Am. In “Yesterday” (in F), the Em7 – A7 – Dm move is a ii – V into Dm, the vi chord: A7 is V7/vi.

Notice the overlap with the previous section: the major II is just V/V that doesn't resolve right away, and the major III is V/vi. Same chords, two ways to hear them.

Line clichés

A line cliché holds a chord and moves one note inside it chromatically, usually down. The chord barely changes; the moving line does all the work.

  • Major: C – Cmaj7 – C7 – F. The root walks C, B, B♭, A (the last step lands on F's 3rd). It's the verse of the Beatles' “Something”.
  • Minor: Am – Am(maj7) – Am7 – Am6. The line is A, G♯, G, F♯. A minor-key ballad staple.

Bass lines: the descending line

Move the moving line to the bass and you get slash chords. The chord stays put (or nearly), the bass walks down step by step, and the harmony seems to slide.

{{chords: Am_open, Am_over_Gsharp, Am_over_G, Am_over_Fsharp, Fmaj7_open}}

That's A – G♯ – G – F♯ – F in the bass under an Am that turns into Fmaj7. The verse of “While My Guitar Gently Weeps” walks its bass down A – G – F♯ – F in the same way. The diatonic version, without the chromatic steps, is in nearly every folk song:

{{grid: C | C/B | Am | Am/G ; title="C – C/B – Am – Am/G: the bass walks C B A G"}} {{grid: G | G/F# | Em | C ; title="G – G/F♯ – Em – C"}} {{chords: C_open, C_over_B, Am_open, Am_over_G, G_open, G_over_Fsharp}} {{why: Why a slash chord is “allowed” to clash}}

G/F♯ has an F♯ in the bass under a G chord — technically a Gmaj7 in third inversion. It doesn't sound like a jazz chord because the ear follows the moving bass line and hears the F♯ as a step on the way to E. Context decides what a chord is.

Spotting outsiders

  • A minor iv or a ♭ chord (♭VII, ♭VI, ♭III): borrowed from the parallel minor.
  • A 7 chord that isn't V7: a secondary dominant — check what's a 5th below it.
  • A major chord where the key has a minor (II, III, VI): a secondary dominant, resolving or not.
  • A chord that changes one note at a time, or a slash chord: a line, in the middle or in the bass.
{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Play G – B – C – Cm slowly. Pick out the D♯ in the B chord and the E♭ in Cm, and follow where each one goes.
  • Play I – ♭VII – IV – I in G (G F C G) and in D (D C G D).
  • Play the Am line cliché and the C line cliché, two beats per chord.
  • Take a progression you know in C and insert A7 before every Dm and E7 before every Am.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

What are iv, ♭VII and ♭VI in the key of D?

Gm, C and B♭.

In the key of G, which chord is V7/vi, and what note gives it away?

B7 (it resolves to Em). Its 3rd, D♯, isn't in G major and leads up to E.

Creep is G – B – C – Cm. Name each chord's function.

I, III (major III, a secondary dominant of vi), IV, iv (borrowed from G minor).

What's the bass line in C – C/B – Am – Am/G?

C, B, A, G — a descending scale.
