--- title: How chords are built order: 30 summary: Stacking thirds, the four triads, and why your open C, A, G, E and D shapes contain exactly the notes they do. level: 1 ---

A chord is three or more notes chosen by one rule: start on a note and skip every other note of the scale. Once you see that rule, every chord name in the next chapter is just the rule applied a little further.

Stacking thirds

Take C major — C D E F G A B — and pick every other note starting from C: C, E, G. Each step skips one letter, so each step is a 3rd. That three-note stack is a triad, and its notes are called the root (C), the 3rd (E) and the 5th (G), named by their distance from the root.

Keep going and you get the 7th (B), then the 9th (D), 11th (F) and 13th (A) — Chapter 4 covers those. For now: three notes, two stacked 3rds.

The four triads

A 3rd is either major (4 half steps) or minor (3). Two stacked 3rds give four combinations, and those are the four triads:

TriadBottom 3rdTop 3rdFormulaOn CSound
Majormajorminor1 3 5C E Gbright, settled
Minorminormajor1 ♭3 5C E♭ Gdarker, still settled
Diminishedminorminor1 ♭3 ♭5C E♭ G♭tense, wants to move
Augmentedmajormajor1 3 ♯5C E G♯unresolved, dreamy

Major and minor both have a perfect 5th on top (7 half steps from the root); they differ only in the 3rd. That's why the 3rd is the note that decides a chord's colour, and why it's the note you'll keep hunting for in every shape you learn.

{{neck: notes="C E G" frets=0-12 labels=intervals title="C major triad: every C, E and G to fret 12"}} {{why: Why “diminished” and “augmented”?}}

They're named after their 5th. A diminished triad's 5th is a half step smaller than perfect (♭5): diminished means made smaller. An augmented triad's 5th is a half step larger (♯5): augmented means made larger. The 3rds don't appear in the name at all.

{{/why}}

Doubling: why six strings, three notes

A guitar chord usually has four to six strings ringing but only three different notes. The rest are doublings — the same notes in another octave. Doubling the root makes a chord solid; doubling the 3rd makes it sweeter and slightly softer; dropping the 5th entirely barely changes the chord's identity. When a shape gets awkward, the 5th is the first note to go.

Dissecting your open chords

Here are the five open major chords you've played for years, with the interval each string plays printed underneath. Read them string by string.

{{chord: C_open}}

C: root on string 5 (fret 3), 3rd on string 4 (E), open G string is the 5th, root again on string 2, 3rd again on the open top string. Two 3rds — that's part of why open C sounds so sweet.

{{chord: A_open}}

A: open root on string 5, then 5th, root, 3rd on string 2 (C♯ under finger 3), 5th on the open top string. Only one 3rd, high in the chord. Move that one finger and the chord changes character: that's Chapter 5.

{{chord: G_open}}

G: root on string 6, 3rd on string 5 (B), open D is the 5th, open G is the root, open B is the 3rd again, and the top string is the root. Three roots: the heaviest, most grounded of the open chords.

{{chord: E_open}}

E: root, 5th, root, 3rd on string 3 (G♯ under finger 1), 5th, root. One 3rd, buried in the middle; everything else is root and 5th. Lift finger 1 and the open G string gives you a ♭3: Em.

{{chord: D_open}}

D: open root on string 4, 5th on string 3, root on string 2, 3rd on string 1 (F♯ under finger 2). The 3rd is the highest note, so it's what you hear on top. Drop it one fret (F) and you have Dm.

{{why: Why “CAGED”?}}

These five shapes — C, A, G, E, D — are the five open major shapes that use the open strings fully (F and B need a barre), and each can be moved up the neck with a barre or the index finger playing the role of the nut. In that order they also join up end to end along the neck. Chapter 9 shows how.

{{/why}}

Major to minor: one note

Every minor chord you know is its major shape with the 3rd lowered a half step:

  • E → Em: finger 1 comes off string 3 (G♯ → G).
  • A → Am: string 2 drops from fret 2 to fret 1 (C♯ → C), and the hand reshapes.
  • D → Dm: string 1 drops from fret 2 to fret 1 (F♯ → F).
{{chords: E_open, Em_open, A_open, Am_open, D_open, Dm_open}}

There's no open C minor or G minor that's comfortable, because in those shapes the 3rd is on a string you'd have to fret behind the nut. That's the first reason barre chords exist.

Inversions in one sentence

A chord's identity comes from its notes; its bass note is a separate choice. With the root lowest, a chord is in root position; with the 3rd lowest it's in first inversion (written C/E); with the 5th lowest, second inversion (C/G). Same chord, different weight. Chapter 12 builds on this.

{{chords: C_open, C_over_E, C_over_G}} {{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Strum each open chord, then pick only the string that carries its 3rd. Say “three” as you pick it.
  • Play E–Em, A–Am, D–Dm and listen to the 3rd change and nothing else.
  • Play C, then C/E (let the low E ring), then C/G. Hear the bass change while the chord stays the same.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

What's the formula of a minor triad, and its notes on A?

1 ♭3 5: A C E.

Which interval do major and minor triads share, and which do they differ by?

Both have a perfect 5th from the root. They differ only in the 3rd: major (4 half steps) or minor (3).

In open G (320003), which strings play the 3rd?

String 5 (B at fret 2) and the open B string.

A chord has the notes C E G and E is the lowest. What is it called?

C/E — C major in first inversion.
