Major and minor chords are stable: they can end a song. The chords in this chapter can't. Each contains an interval that wants to move — a tritone, an augmented 5th, a note rubbing a half step against another — and that pull is exactly what makes them useful. Used one beat at a time, they make plain progressions move.

The tritone: where tension comes from

The tritone is six half steps — half an octave. In a G7 chord it's between B and F, the 3rd and the ♭7. Resolve G7 to C and B rises a half step to C while F falls a half step to E. That contrary half-step motion is the strongest pull in tonal music, and most tension chords contain a tritone or something that imitates its pull.

Diminished 7th: the symmetrical chord

A dim7 chord is minor 3rds all the way up: C – E♭ – G♭ – B𝄫(A) – and the next minor 3rd is C again. It cuts the octave into four equal parts. Consequences:

  • Every note can be the root. Cdim7, E♭dim7, G♭dim7 and Adim7 are the same four notes.
  • Move any dim7 shape three frets and you have the same chord again, rearranged.
  • There are only three different dim7 chords in all of music: the ones on C, C♯ and D. Everything else is one of those, respelled.
{{chords: Gdim7_barre_E3, Cdim7_barre_A3, Fdim7_s4_3, Ddim7_open}}

Each dim7 contains two tritones (C–G♭ and E♭–A), which is why it sounds so unstable.

Using dim7 as a passing chord

The classic use: between two chords whose roots are a whole step apart, put a dim7 on the note in between. The bass rises by half steps, and every note of the dim7 is a half step from a note of the target.

{{grid: C | C#dim7 | Dm | G7 ; title="I – ♯I°7 – ii – V7 in C"}} {{grid: G | G#dim7 | Am7 | D7 ; title="The same in G"}}

It also works descending (E♭dim7 between E and D) and as a decorative chord over a held bass: G – G°7 – G, with the dim7 on the same root, is a vintage-sounding shimmer.

{{why: Why a dim7 can replace a V7}}

Take G7♭9: G B D F A♭. Remove the root and the remaining B D F A♭ is Bdim7. So Bdim7 → C sounds like G7 → C, with extra bite. Any dim7 a half step below the target chord's root works as its dominant. That's why C♯dim7 leads so naturally to Dm: it's A7♭9 without the A.

{{/why}}

Half-diminished (m7♭5)

A diminished triad plus a ♭7, not a 𝄫7: B – D – F – A. It's the natural seventh chord on the 7th degree of a major key, and — more usefully — on the 2nd degree of a minor key. That gives it a fixed job: the ii in a minor ii–V–i.

{{grid: Bm7b5 | E7 | Am | Am ; title="iiø7 – V7 – i in A minor"}} {{chords: Bm7b5_barre_A2, E7_open, Am_open, Gm7b5_barre_E3, Cm7b5_barre_A3}}

In pop it's also a softer stand-in for iv: in C, Dm7♭5 (D F A♭ C) is Fm6 in another inversion and does the same bittersweet job.

Augmented

Major 3rds all the way up: C – E – G♯ – and the next major 3rd is C again. Like dim7, it's symmetrical: move it four frets and it's the same chord, and there are only four different augmented triads.

{{chords: Caug_open, Daug_open, Gaug_barre_E3}}

Its classic job is as a raised-5th passing chord: the 5th of a chord rises by half steps while the rest holds.

  • I – I+ – vi or IV: C – Caug – Am or F. The G rises to G♯ and then A.
  • V+ – I: the augmented V's ♯5 leads up to the 3rd of I. The Beatles' “Oh! Darling” opens on an augmented chord for exactly this lift into the verse.
{{grid: C | Caug | Am | F ; title="C – C+ – Am – F: the line G, G♯, A"}}

Ninths, elevenths, thirteenths

Extensions add colour on top of a 7th chord without changing its function. A C9 does the same job as C7; it just sounds richer. The guitar can't play every note of a big extended chord, so voicings leave things out, in this order:

  1. The 5th — almost always first.
  2. The root — if a bass player has it.
  3. In 13 chords, the 11th (it clashes with the 3rd) and often the 9th.
  4. In 11 chords, the 3rd — so an “11” on guitar is usually a 9sus4 or a ♭VII triad over the root (B♭/C for C11).

Never drop the 3rd or the 7th of a 9 or 13 chord: they define its quality (Chapter 15).

7♯9 and altered dominants in rock

A 7♯9 has a major 3rd and, an octave up, a note that sounds like the minor 3rd. Major and minor at once: the blues in one chord. On guitar it's a four-note shape with the root on string 5.

{{chord: E7sharp9_barre_A7}}

It's “Purple Haze” (E7♯9) and the Beatles' “Taxman” (D7♯9). Slide the shape so the square is on your root: fret 5 for D, fret 3 for C.

Other altered dominants that turn up in rock and pop:

  • 7♭9: play a dim7 shape on the 3rd of the chord (for E7♭9, G♯dim7). Spanish and gypsy-jazz flavour.
  • 7♯5 (aug7): V+7 → I, the ♯5 rising to the 3rd of I. Old-time, Beatles-era pop endings.
  • 13: the funk and soul dominant. Root, ♭7, 3, 13 — four notes, one shape.
{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Play the dim7 shape at frets 3, 6, 9 and 12 and hear that it's the same chord.
  • Play C – C♯dim7 – Dm – G7 and G – G♯dim7 – Am7 – D7, two beats each.
  • Play Bm7♭5 – E7 – Am and loop it.
  • Play E7, then E9, then E13, then E7♯9 over an open low E. Name what each adds.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

Why is moving a dim7 shape three frets the same chord?

A dim7 is four minor 3rds stacked to fill the octave. Moving it three frets (a minor 3rd) shifts every note onto the next note of the same chord.

What's the passing dim7 between G and Am?

G♯dim7 (G♯ B D F) — the bass rises G, G♯, A.

Spell Am7♭5 and say where it occurs.

A C E♭ G. It's the ii chord of G minor (and the vii of B♭ major).

In a 13 chord on guitar, which notes are usually left out, and which must stay?

Usually the 5th, 9th and 11th (and sometimes the root) are left out. The 3rd and ♭7 must stay; they make it a dominant chord.
