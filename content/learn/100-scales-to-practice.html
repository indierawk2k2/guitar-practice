--- title: Scales to practise order: 100 summary: The major scale in five CAGED positions, the five minor pentatonic boxes, major pentatonic as the same boxes re-rooted, natural minor — and how to practise scales so they turn into music. level: 3 ---

A scale is the key from Chapter 7 laid out as notes to play. Scales on guitar are worth practising for two reasons: they teach your hand the shape of a key everywhere on the neck, and they give you the raw material for riffs, fills and melodies. Neither benefit comes from running them up and down as fast as possible. This chapter gives you the shapes and then a better way to use them.

Positions, not patterns

A position is a four- or five-fret region of the neck where the hand stays put, one finger per fret, and plays every note of the scale available there. The major scale divides into five positions, one around each CAGED chord shape from Chapter 10. Learn each as “the scale around this chord shape”, and you'll always know where the roots are.

E minor: the same notes, a different home

E natural minor uses exactly the notes of G major, its relative major (Chapter 7). The dots don't move; the squares do. Every G major position is also an E minor position — you just treat E as home and lean on Em, Am and Bm shapes instead of G, C and D.

{{scale: E_minor_neck}}

The formula for natural minor is 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7: a major scale with the 3rd, 6th and 7th lowered. Here it is as one position in A, built around the E-shape Am barre:

Minor pentatonic: five boxes

The pentatonic scale is the natural minor with the two notes that clash most easily — the 2 and the ♭6 — removed: 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7. Five notes, two per string in every box, nothing that sounds wrong over a minor or bluesy chord. It's the scale most rock guitar is made of.

{{scale: A_minor_pent_pos1}} {{scale: A_minor_pent_pos2}} {{scale: A_minor_pent_pos3}} {{scale: A_minor_pent_pos4}} {{scale: A_minor_pent_pos5}} {{scale: A_minor_pent_neck}}

Box 1 sits around the E-shape Am barre; box 4 around the A-shape Am at fret 12; box 5 sits just below box 1 and is the same as box 5 an octave up at frets 14–17. The five boxes are the five CAGED regions again, with notes removed.

{{scale: A_blues_pos1}}

Add the ♭5 and you have the blues scale. Use the ♭5 as a passing note between 4 and 5, not a note to land on.

Major pentatonic: the same boxes, re-rooted

A minor pentatonic (A C D E G) and C major pentatonic (C D E G A) are the same five notes. So the five boxes are also the five boxes of C major pentatonic — only the roots change. Box 2 of A minor is box 1 of C major.

{{scale: C_major_pent_neck}}

That gives a fast rule for any major key: play the minor pentatonic of the relative minor — three frets below the major root. For G major pentatonic, play E minor pentatonic box 1 at fret 0; for A major, F♯ minor pentatonic at fret 2. The most-used major pentatonic shape is the next box up, with the root on string 6 under the second finger:

{{scale: G_major_pent_pos1}}

Major pentatonic (1 2 3 5 6) is the sweet, open sound of country, soul and a lot of jangly guitar pop. Minor pentatonic over a major chord is the bluesy, tougher sound. The same box played over different chords changes character completely — which is the point of the next section.

How to practise scales musically

Running a scale up and down trains one path through it. Music uses every path. Use these instead, slowly, with a metronome:

  • Say the roots. Each time you hit a square, say the note name. Then say every interval as you play it: “one, two, three…”. Ten minutes of this teaches more than an hour of silent runs.
  • Sequences. Play in groups: 1-2-3, 2-3-4, 3-4-5… then groups of four. Then in 3rds: 1-3, 2-4, 3-5, 4-6… Sequences force you to know where each note is, not just what comes next.
  • Start anywhere. Start the position on its highest note and go down; start in the middle; stop on a root and resolve.
  • Over a chord. Record a minute of a single chord (or loop two: G and C, Am and D). Play the scale over it slowly and listen to which notes sound stable (chord tones: 1, 3, 5) and which want to move. Land phrases on chord tones.
  • Sing it. Play a note, sing it, play the next. Even badly. Singing ties the shape to your ear, which is where melodies come from.
  • Make a phrase. Four notes and a rest. Repeat it, change one note, repeat. That's improvising, and it's the end goal of scale practice.
{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Learn G major, E-shape position: say each interval as you play it, up and down, at 60 bpm.
  • Play A minor pentatonic box 1 in groups of three, then over a looped Am chord, ending every phrase on A.
  • Play the same box 1 shape at fret 2 (F♯ minor pentatonic) over an A chord. Hear it turn into A major pentatonic.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

What is the minor pentatonic formula, and which two notes of the natural minor does it leave out?

1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7. It leaves out the 2 and the ♭6.

Why can one set of dots be both G major and E minor?

They're relative keys: the same seven notes. Only the home note (the root you treat as stable) changes.

You want D major pentatonic. Which minor pentatonic box 1 do you play, and where?

B minor pentatonic (the relative minor), box 1 at fret 7.

Name two ways to practise a scale that aren't straight up-and-down runs.

Any two of: saying intervals/roots aloud, sequences (groups of three or four, or in 3rds), starting from different notes, playing over a looped chord and landing on chord tones, singing, making short phrases.
