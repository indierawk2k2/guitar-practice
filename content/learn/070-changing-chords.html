Knowing a chord and changing to it in time are two different skills. The first is a shape; the second is a movement, and movements are learned differently. This chapter is about the movement: what's going on when a new shape feels impossible, how to analyse any change, and a set of named drills that turn hard changes into boring ones.

Why new shapes feel impossible

When you play G, you don't place four fingers. You send one command — “G” — and the hand assembles itself. Thirty years of repetition have fused the separate finger movements into one motor chunk, stored and triggered as a unit.

A new shape has no chunk yet. Every finger is a separate decision that needs attention, in sequence, while the old chunks keep firing on their own and pulling your fingers toward familiar places. That's why an unfamiliar voicing — a thumb-over D/F♯, say, or a Bm7(add11) with open strings between the fingers — can feel clumsier than anything you've struggled with in decades. It's not lost dexterity. It's the normal state of an unlearned movement, and you haven't been in that state for a long time.

Three things are well established about how that changes:

  • It consolidates between sessions. A lot of motor learning happens offline, especially during sleep. A shape that was a struggle at night is often noticeably easier the next day without any practice in between. Several short sessions on consecutive days beat one long session.
  • Correct repetitions are what count. The hand learns whatever it does repeatedly, including the fumble. Ten slow, correct placements teach more than fifty rushed ones with buzzes and corrections.
  • The change has to be learned separately from the chord. You can hold Bm perfectly and still not get to it from G in time, because “G-to-Bm” is its own movement.

How many reps? There's no magic number. Expect a few hundred correct placements, spread over one to three weeks, before a genuinely new shape lands without thought — and a similar amount for each change into it that you care about. That sounds like a lot; at one clean change every few seconds it's a few minutes a day.

The change is the unit

Practise the transition, not the chord. A song is a chain of changes; most of them you already own. Find the one or two that break — that's where the practice goes. The rest of this chapter is tools for breaking a single change down and building it back up.

Read the change before you play it

Before practising any change, look at both shapes and find out what doesn't have to move.

  • Common tones / anchor fingers: a finger on the same string and fret in both chords. Leave it down. It's free accuracy and it locates the rest of the hand.
  • Guide fingers: a finger on the same string in both chords, at a different fret. Don't lift it — slide it along the string, keeping light contact. It drags the hand into place.
  • Pivot shapes: fingers that keep the same relationship to each other and move together as a block, like a power-chord pair or a whole barre shape sliding up the neck.
  • Re-fingering: sometimes the change gets easier if you finger one chord differently. Play A with fingers 1-2-3 and the ring finger can guide from A into D (it stays on string 2 and slides from fret 2 to fret 3).

Anchors in action

The rock G family shares two anchor fingers. Fingers 3 and 4 stay on fret 3 of strings 2 and 1 through all four of these; only fingers 1 and 2 move.

Move the hand as one shape

  • Lift together, land together. Placing fingers one at a time is the habit to break. Lift the whole hand a few millimetres, make the next shape in the air on the way, and land all fingers at once.
  • Stay close. Fingers that fly a few centimetres off the strings have that far to come back. Hover; lift only enough to stop the strings sounding.
  • Lead with the bass. A downstroke hits the low strings first. Get the finger for the lowest fretted string down on the beat; the treble fingers have a few milliseconds more while the pick travels across. In G → C, the ring finger to string 5 is the one that has to be on time.
  • Leave early. On the last eighth note before a change (the “and” of 4), let go of the old chord and strum whatever's open while you travel. In a band, or even solo at tempo, that half-beat of open strings is nearly inaudible, and it gives you an eighth note of travel time. Players who sound effortless do this all the time.

Slow motion and the metronome

Speed comes from accuracy, not the other way round. The metronome rule that works:

  1. Find the tempo where the change is perfect — every note clean, no hesitation — five times in a row. Often that's slower than feels reasonable: 50–60 bpm, one chord per bar.
  2. After three clean passes, go up 4 bpm.
  3. After a miss, drop back 8 bpm and do three clean passes there.
  4. When one chord per bar is easy at 90+, halve the space: two beats each, back at the slow tempo. Then one beat each.

Write today's top clean tempo in the packet. The number climbing over a week is the best motivation there is.

Barres: tension, thumb and arm

Most barre trouble is too much squeezing in the wrong place.

  • Thumb low and behind. Put the thumb pad on the back of the neck roughly behind the middle finger, lower than for open chords. Not hooked over the top.
  • Pull, don't pinch. The pressure should come mostly from the weight of the arm pulling the hand back toward you, not from the thumb clamping. If the thumb aches, you're pinching.
  • Roll the index slightly onto its thumb-side edge — it's bonier and firmer than the soft underside — and keep it right behind the fret.
  • Press only where notes are. In an E-shape barre the index only needs to sound strings 6, 2 and 1; the other fingers cover the rest. The barre doesn't have to be equally strong everywhere.
  • Find the minimum. Press until the chord rings, then slowly ease off until it just starts to buzz, then add a little back. That's the pressure to play with. Most people use two or three times more than they need.
  • Check the guitar. High action or old strings make barres much harder. A setup is cheap compared with the frustration.

When to stop

  • Sharp pain, tingling or numbness in the hand, wrist or forearm: stop for the day. That's not something to push through.
  • Burning fatigue in the forearm muscles: rest a few minutes, shake out, do something easy, come back.
  • Accuracy falling off: once more than one in three changes is sloppy, you're practising the sloppy version. Switch to something else.
  • Time box new shapes. Ten to fifteen focused minutes on a hard change per session, daily, does more than an hour once a week. Stop while it's still going well.

The drills

The practice packets refer to these by name. Each takes one or two minutes; pick the one that matches the problem.

One-minute changes one-minute
Two chords, one minute on a timer, change back and forth as fast as you can while keeping every change clean; strum once per chord. Count the changes and write the number down. The daily benchmark for any pair: the number is your progress chart.
Air shapes air-shape
Take the hand off the neck. Form the chord in the air an inch above the strings, then land all fingers at once. Check, lift, repeat ten times. Use it when: a new shape that you build finger by finger.
Place and lift place-lift
Fret the chord, release pressure until the fingers just touch the strings, press again. No strumming. Twenty presses. Use it when: a shape that collapses under pressure; barres.
Slow motion slow-motion
Make the change as slowly as you can — five seconds — with every finger moving at once along the shortest path. Watch the fingers. Use it when: a change where fingers tangle or arrive in the wrong order.
Anchor hunt anchor
Before practising, name the anchor, guide and moving fingers for the change. Play it ten times keeping anchors down and guides sliding. Use it when: any new change, first.
Bass first bass-first
Play only the root of the new chord on beat 1, then strum the rest. Then land the bass finger first in a full strum. Use it when: changes that land late or with the wrong bass.
Leave early leave-early
Four down-up bars. On the last “and” of each bar, lift off and strum open strings while moving. Use it when: changes that make you stop the strumming hand.
Change loop change-loop
One strum on chord A, one on chord B, release, reset, repeat — the transition only, no music around it. Ten clean in a row. Use it when: the one change in a song that breaks.
Tempo ladder tempo-ladder
The metronome rule: start where it's perfect, +4 bpm after three clean passes, −8 after a miss. Record the top clean tempo. Use it when: any change you can do slowly but not in time.
Eyes closed eyes-closed
Do the change without looking. Open your eyes to check after landing. Use it when: a shape you can play but only while watching the fretboard.
Barre pulse barre-pulse
Hold a barre; on each click (60 bpm) relax to the buzz point, then press to clean. Thumb light throughout. Use it when: barres that hurt or tire you quickly.
Random pairs random-pairs
Write 4–6 chords on cards. Draw two, do one-minute changes, draw again. Use it when: making a set of shapes independent of any one song.
Song loop song-loop
Loop the two bars containing the hard change, in the song's real rhythm, at a slow tempo, then add a bar either side. Use it when: putting an isolated change back into music.

A recipe for any hard change

  1. Anchor hunt — know what stays and what slides.
  2. Air shapes on the new chord until it lands whole.
  3. Slow motion, then change loop — ten clean in a row.
  4. Tempo ladder one chord per bar, with leave early once it's near tempo.
  5. One-minute changes as the daily number.
  6. Song loop to put it back in the song.

Do it for ten minutes, stop, come back tomorrow. The day-to-day improvement is the consolidation doing its work.

{{try: What to take to the guitar}}
  • Pick the one change you find hardest in a song you play. Do an anchor hunt on it before playing a note.
  • Run the recipe above on it for ten minutes; write down the one-minute number. Repeat tomorrow and compare.
  • Play G–C–D–G at a comfortable tempo using leave-early on every change.
  • Hold an F barre and find its minimum pressure. Notice how much less it is than you were using.
{{/try}}

Check yourself

What's the difference between an anchor finger and a guide finger?

An anchor stays on the same string and fret in both chords, so it never moves. A guide stays on the same string but changes fret, so it slides without lifting.

Why does a hard change often feel easier the day after you practise it?

Motor learning consolidates between sessions, much of it during sleep. Short daily sessions use that; one long session doesn't.

What's the metronome rule?

Start at a tempo where the change is perfect; go up 4 bpm after three clean passes; drop 8 bpm after a miss.

In a barre chord, where should most of the pressure come from?

The weight of the arm pulling back, not the thumb squeezing. Thumb low behind the neck, index rolled slightly onto its edge, and only as much pressure as it takes to ring clean.
